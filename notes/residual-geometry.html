The Residual Stream Has a Geometry of Time

mechanistic interpretability
residual stream
transformers
A preliminary Gemma-2-2B pilot finds a low-dimensional residual-stream subspace whose directions remain slow across tokens, supported by broad high-variance geometry but distinct from PCA axes.
Published

May 21, 2026

Preface

This is a preliminary writeup for an experiment on residual stream geometry. The research direction seems pretty underexplored, so I’m posting early to collect objections, research intuitions, and connections to problems other people are thinking about before I invest in the larger run.

The case for skimming this post: this pilot suggests we may be in a world where transformers keep track of context in a surprisingly compact way. Information that persists across many tokens is not diffuse across activation space; it concentrates in a low-dimensional subspace that can be projected out, compared to attention/MLP writes, and potentially targeted by interventions.

One reason I am posting before the larger runs is to de-risk the continuations. One of the next experiments applies the same pipeline to an abstract-reasoning corpus with labeled running variables, to test whether the slow subspace carries variables the model updates mid-computation rather than only web-document register. The current method has specific limits: lexical clustering can mimic maintained state, autocorrelation alone cannot distinguish held state from an evolving-but-decodable variable, and a fixed-direction probe can miss a variable that rotates through representation space. I sketch the controls and a held-versus-evolving discriminator in Continuations; design feedback there would be especially useful.

Summary

  • The residual stream is commonly analogized to the transformer’s “working memory”: at each token position, a high-dimensional vector accumulates the attention and MLP deltas. This picture considers state transformations along the depth-time axis, i.e. layer by layer.

  • There is a second axis of sequence-time. Within a layer, the model must also keep track of information at position \(t\) which is useful at position \(t + k\). This experiment aims to discover the geometry of how the model tracks state across tokens.

  • A direction’s timescale \(\tau_j\) is the lag at which its sample autocorrelation first drops below \(1/e \approx 0.37\). To calculate \(\tau_j\) for direction \(v_j\), I project the residual stream onto \(\hat v_j\) at every token position, compute within-document autocorrelation curves, average them over documents to estimate lag-k autocorrelation \(R_j(k)\), and set \(\tau_j = \min\{k \geq 1 : R_j(k) < 1/e\}\). Estimator details are in Appendix A.

  • The experiment compares three probe families: 512 random directions (null baseline), 256 PCA directions (variance-ranked), and 256 time-lagged probes (multi-lag TICA-style, persistence-ranked).

  • I estimated the distribution of timescales across residual-stream directions in layer 12 of Gemma-2-2B on 5,000 C4 documents, then investigated the properties of the high-\(\tau\) directions: where they live in the ambient space, how many there are, what they appear to semantically encode, and whether their high timescales are tied to sequential context or just unigram statistics.

  • The result is a low-dimensional slow subspace (about 31 directions, roughly 5 effective slow dimensions) in which a generic direction is slow while a generic direction in the matched-dimension PCA-31 comparator is not. It is supported by the high-variance residual substrate but oriented away from the PCA axes, it is attention-aligned beyond residual-PCA controls, and on C4 it reads as durable web-document state rather than abstract reasoning variables. Whether it carries abstract maintained variables is one question for the next experiment, on a reasoning corpus.

What Was Found

Finding 1: The timescale distribution is extremely heavy-tailed. Random and PCA directions have a 90th-percentile timescale of 1 token, carrying essentially no signal across positions. Time-lagged probes have a 90th-percentile timescale of 17 tokens.

Finding 2: State timescale is not a corpus artifact. Shuffling token positions within documents, which preserves the token multiset but destroys sequential order, collapses the top-decile timescale of slow probes from 17 tokens to 1 (94% reduction). It addresses the confound that a high \(\tau\) could result from recurring token clusters rather than a maintained state tracked by the direction.

Where the slow directions live: held-out projection collapse

Findings 1 and 2 establish that slow directions exist and that their persistence reflects sequential order. The next two questions are where in the residual stream they live and how many of them there are. I answer the first with a projection-collapse test.

I build three \(k\)-dimensional bases, project each out of the residual stream, and recompute timescales on held-out probes. The slow-direction basis is the test of interest; the top residual PCA basis tests whether the slow subspace functionally depends on generic high-variance residual geometry; the random orthonormal basis tests whether deleting any \(k\) dimensions would reduce persistence. The test is constructed to be non-circular: the probes used to build the projected basis and the probes used to evaluate it are fit on disjoint train shards, and evaluated on the same held-out documents before and after projection. Details on the anti-circularity construction are in Appendix B.

At \(k = 256\) (test split), held-out persistence collapse was \(100\%\) for the slow-direction basis, \(99.85\%\) for the PCA basis, and \(9.70\%\) for the random control. The plotted curves show the test split; validation was evaluated and agrees. So projecting out the top PCA directions removes held-out persistence almost as completely as projecting out the selected slow directions themselves, while removing the same number of random directions barely touches it.

Finding 3: the slow subspace is functionally supported by the broad high-variance residual substrate. Removing the PCA-256 basis collapses held-out persistence, and removing a matched random 256-dimensional basis does not. The slow subspace therefore depends on the high-variance substrate, not on a quiet low-variance corner that variance-ranked methods would miss. This is a statement about functional dependence, not exact geometric containment: as the geometry in Finding 5 and Appendix E shows, the slow basis is only about half captured by PCA-256, so removing that substrate destroys the temporal state without the substrate reconstructing the slow basis exactly. Functional deletion and geometric containment are related but distinct metrics. What the next finding establishes is that the slow subspace is low-dimensional and is not the PCA frame.

Finding 4: the slow subspace is low-dimensional and distinct from the PCA axes. Two facts separate the slow directions from the high-variance residual substrate. First, the PCA axes are individually short-lived: a top PCA direction has median \(\tau = 1\) (Finding 1), so high variance is not itself enough for slowness. Second, the slow directions concentrate into a small number of specific rotations. Define a direction’s timescale excess as its timescale above the random-direction median,

\[ \Delta \tau_j = \max\!\left(\tau_j - \operatorname{median}_{u \in \mathrm{random}}\tau_u,\ 0\right), \]

and sort the excesses \(\Delta\tau_{(1)} \geq \cdots \geq \Delta\tau_{(1024)}\) across the deduplicated eligible probe set. In this pilot,

\[ \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{31}\Delta\tau_{(i)}}{\sum_{i=1}^{1024}\Delta\tau_{(i)}} \approx 0.8, \]

so 31 directions carry roughly 80% of the total timescale excess. These directions are nearly orthogonal: median pairwise absolute cosine is \(0.035\), and effective rank is about 28 of 31. For scale, two independent random unit vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^{2304}\) have \(\operatorname{sd}(\langle u,v\rangle) = 1/\sqrt{2304} \approx 0.0208\), so the observed median cosine is only about 1.7x the random-cosine SD.

This establishes 31 distinct slow directions. It does not yet establish a subspace: a set of slow axes is not the same as a region in which a generic direction is slow. That is the question the next section settles.

Is the whole span slow, or just the selected frame?

Finding 5: A generic direction inside the selected span is slow, not only the directions the objective selected. If only the selected frame is slow, the result is 31 special axes. If a randomly chosen direction in the span is slow too, the result is a slow subspace.

To test this I sampled random unit vectors inside the span of the 31 directions (Gaussian in the 31-dimensional basis, mapped to the ambient space, unit-normalized) and computed each one’s held-out timescale with the same estimator used throughout. The comparison is against random unit vectors drawn inside the top-\(k\) PCA subspaces (PCA-\(k\)) for \(k \in \{31, 128, 256\}\), and against ambient-random directions. Method and full tables are in Appendix E.

The contrast is large. A generic direction in the slow subspace has median \(\tau = 24\) on validation (\(25\) on test). A generic direction in the matched-dimension PCA comparator, PCA-31, has median \(\tau = 1.5\); in PCA-128 and PCA-256, \(1.0\); ambient-random, \(1.0\). Same dimension, opposite behavior. And the slow subspace is not the top-PCA frame: its 31-dimensional basis has only 2.79 effective dimensions of overlap with PCA-31 and 16.14 with PCA-256, with about 48% of its energy outside PCA-256 entirely (Appendix E). So persistence is not a property of the leading PCA axes, nor of generic directions in PCA subspaces; it is a structured rotation, diffuse across a broad high-variance substrate, that the time-lagged objective recovers and the variance ordering does not.

The separation holds at the bottom of the distribution, not just at the median. Every one of 512 directions sampled from the slow subspace exceeded the ambient null band, with the 5th percentile at \(\tau = 10\), roughly seven times the null. The PCA controls fail this test: half of random PCA-31 directions fall in the null band, and 85% of PCA-256 directions do. So the median is not propped up by a few slow members. There is no fast corner of the slow subspace that the sampling found. The honest limit on this is that 512 samples support a claim about generic directions, not a proof that every direction in the continuous subspace is slow.

The slow subspace is slow throughout but not uniformly slow. Sweeping its dimension shows a steep internal gradient: a random direction in the top-1 span has median \(\tau = 492\), top-2 = \(298\), top-5 = \(116\), top-13 = \(30\), settling to \(25\) by the full 31 (test split; validation tracks within a token or two). Part of this decline is mechanical, since a larger span includes progressively less-slow directions and a random vector places less of its energy on the slowest modes. The substantive facts are that the floor stays far above the null, and that random mixes of only the bottom-ranked directions (ranks 17 to 31, excluding all of the slowest) still have median \(\tau = 14\). So the tail is independently slow rather than noise. Quantitatively, the 31 directions have a geometric participation ratio of \(28\) (they point in roughly 28 distinct directions) but a slowness participation ratio of about \(5\) (the persistence concentrates into roughly five effective dimensions, with one dominant ultra-slow mode).

The structure is stable across splits. The 31 selected directions have median \(\tau\) of \(17\), \(16\), and \(17\) on train, validation, and test, with Spearman rank correlation above \(0.99\) across every split pair and no shrinkage from train to test. The random-in-span directions reproduce direction by direction across validation and test (Spearman \(0.996\), median absolute \(\tau\) change of 1 token), and every one exceeds the null on both splits. The slow subspace is not a fit-shard artifact.

This is the result the rest of the post rests on. The time-lagged objective recovers a low-dimensional slow subspace, supported by the high-variance residual substrate but oriented away from the PCA axes, in which a generic direction is slow (median \(\tau \approx 25\), none in the null band) while a generic direction in the matched-dimension PCA-31 comparator is not (median \(\tau = 1.5\)). What PCA orders by variance, this orders by persistence, and the two orderings select different directions.

What the directions encode (exploratory)

This section is a qualitative validation-split readout of saved projections. It is hypothesis generation, not a locked result.

The goal is to characterize what the slow subspace carries. The risk in any such readout is cheap storytelling: given a direction and its top-activating tokens, a plausible label is almost always available, including for directions that are not actually slow. So the load-bearing evidence is not the labels. It is whether generic directions inside the slow subspace sustain high projection over long token runs across the corpus. For each direction I find the token runs where its projection sits in the top 5% (q95), keep runs of at least 8 tokens, and count the distinct documents containing at least one such run, collapsing repeated runs from a single page so that one over-represented document cannot inflate the count.

The contrast is large and survives deduplication. A generic rotation inside the slow subspace sustains a qualifying run in a median of 126 of the 500 validation documents (a fraction of 0.25), and 106 even at the 10th percentile. The matched controls collapse: random PCA-31 reaches 18 documents at the median and under 5 at the 10th percentile, with PCA-128, PCA-256, and ambient-random lower still, a fraction of 0.01 to 0.04 against the slow subspace’s 0.25. The controls do produce colorful top and bottom examples, but their runs are short and confined to a handful of documents. Qualitative examples alone would not separate these families; the document-level run statistics do.

A window-independence check guards the remaining worry: that a direction’s runs all come from one over-represented page, inflating the count with no corpus-wide effect. The distinct-document count of 126 already rules that out. Separately, slow-subspace directions touch fewer distinct top-window documents than ambient-random ones (median 7 versus 12), which is the expected signature of document-level tracking rather than scattered token firing, not the inflation worry resurfacing. The selected probes are sharper than generic rotations within a document (about 4.9 retained runs per covered document versus 3.8 for random-in-span and near 1 for the controls), the same graded structure the participation ratios quantified: the objective picks the sharpest axes, and generic directions in their span are slower but softer.

With the persistence established, the labels are useful only for navigation. Reading the high- and low-projection tails of each direction, the recurring patterns are broad composite axes over register, domain, source template, and page format: formal technical, scientific, institutional, and policy prose on one side against captions or template fragments on the other; travel, hospitality, food, sports, and personal narrative; legal, terms-of-service, commercial, and social-web pages; and scraped catalogs, concatenated listings, and repeated page templates. These are coarse regex summaries of tail examples, not feature names, and some directions visibly track scraped-source or formatting structure rather than meaning.

Illustrative navigation only. These coarse labels summarize projection-tail examples; they are not the evidence for corpus-wide persistence.

The honest verdict is that on C4 the slow subspace carries durable web-document state, not clean semantic features and not abstract reasoning variables. Register, domain, formatting, and source-template structure all contribute, and C4’s scraping artifacts are part of the observed geometry. This is held state: a direction lit steadily over a coherent passage, which is exactly what document-state should look like. It is not evidence that any direction tracks a variable the model is updating as it computes; distinguishing held state from evolving-but-decodable state is a separate test that this corpus cannot answer, and it is taken up in the next steps.

Caveats specific to this section: it inspects ten sampled vectors per span family on validation only; the regex labels are coarse and their value is the top-versus-bottom contrast, not the names.

Attention-aligned geometry (exploratory)

This and the preceding semantic section are exploratory: a step off the main result, with less validation behind them, reported because they are suggestive rather than settled.

Finding 6: The slow directions align with attention-output geometry beyond residual-PCA controls.

The question is what distinguishes the slow rotations from ordinary high-variance PCA directions. A natural hypothesis is that they are aligned with what attention writes into the residual stream. But raw overlap with attention outputs is not enough on its own: attention block outputs also live in the residual stream and may share generic high-variance geometry with residual PCA.

To control for this I compare each direction’s projection onto attention, MLP, and residual-PCA subspaces of the same dimension. For a unit direction \(v\) and a subspace projection matrix \(P\), the overlap score is \(B_P(v) = \|Pv\|_2\), and I define excess attention overlap and excess MLP overlap relative to a matched residual-PCA subspace:

\[ E_{\mathrm{attn}}(v) = B_{\mathrm{attn}}(v) - B_{\mathrm{resid}}(v), \qquad E_{\mathrm{MLP}}(v) = B_{\mathrm{MLP}}(v) - B_{\mathrm{resid}}(v). \]

Positive excess means a direction overlaps the attention or MLP output subspace more than it overlaps a matched residual-PCA subspace of the same dimension.

The curves show the selected-slow-direction minus random-direction median excess as progressively more residual-PCA components are removed. The top-PCA stress-test comparison is reported in the text below.

The slow directions have positive excess attention overlap. The 31 selected slow directions have median excess attention overlap \(+0.1164\). Random directions have mean excess attention overlap \(0.0206\) with standard deviation \(0.0166\), so on the random-control scale the selected slow directions sit about \(5.8\) standard deviations above the random mean:

\[ \frac{0.1164 - 0.0206}{0.0166} \approx 5.76. \]

The PCA stress test makes the same point from the opposite side. Top residual PCs have higher raw attention overlap than the slow directions, but median \(\tau = 1\) and median excess attention overlap \(-0.3991\), about \(25\) control SDs below the random mean. I do not read this as PCA directions being “anti-attention.” It is partly mechanical: these directions are themselves residual-PCA axes, so the matched residual-PCA term is very large. There is also a weaker late-MLP signal after stronger residual-PCA controls, but attention is the primary and more robust geometric signal.

This is a backward, correlational test. It asks whether long-timescale residual directions lie unusually close to the subspaces attention writes into, and it finds that they do. It does not show that any attention head routes, refreshes, or causally maintains state represented in the slow subspace. A direct transport test is consistent with that caution: the alignment-selected head group beat low-alignment and high-variance controls but did not beat same-layer random-head controls on the locked exclude-self transport metric, so the locked causal gate was not passed. The geometric alignment stands on its own, with the mechanism unproven; establishing causal maintenance is future work.

Interpretation: What This Would Be Good For

The current evidence is one model, one layer, one corpus, and one estimator, so the implications are held loosely. The optimistic version is not that these exact directions are universal, but that transformer residual streams may in general carry a low-dimensional slow-subspace geometry, with the basis varying across models and distributions.

The durable contribution, the part that does not depend on any of the larger bets resolving, is the axis itself. PCA ranks directions by residual-stream variance. Sparse feature methods characterize what is represented at a position. Neither directly ranks directions by what the model maintains across positions. Timescale is a third axis of analysis: a label-free, unsupervised way to surface directions that behave like maintained state without knowing in advance what that state is. That instrument is useful even if the semantics stay mundane.

What This Supports Now

A persistence channel for monitoring. Most interpretability readouts are position-local: they report what is present at a token, not whether a state is stable across a passage or transient. Position-local features can still detect a sustained state token by token, but they do not by themselves distinguish maintenance from recurrence or local activation. A persistence readout adds that temporal channel beside existing tools.

A diagnostic for steering. A steering vector that “makes the model more honest” may shift maintained discourse state, or it may simply bias each token toward honest-sounding completions. Projecting a steering vector onto the slow subspace and its complement gives a first diagnostic for this distinction. Establishing the mechanism would still require targeted interventions.

What Remains Gated

Reading abstract intent. On C4 the slow subspace carries web-document state, not goals or reasoning variables. Whether slow-subspace geometry surfaces abstract maintained variables is a corpus question, taken up below, not something this pilot shows.

Surgical intervention. Decomposing every residual-reading or residual-writing weight into slow and transient parts, such as \(PW\) versus \((I-P)W\), or ablating the slow subspace to remove maintained state, are candidate experiments rather than consequences of the geometry. The locked M3 transport gate was negative, the exploratory head-ablation transport test did not beat same-layer random-head controls, and direction ablation has not been run. Removing these directions may also delete energy shared with the high-variance residual substrate. Any intervention claim needs a separate causal design.

One mechanism hypothesis cuts across both tiers. Because the slow directions are attention-aligned, they may be written or retrieved through attention reading particular keys and values. That would connect residual geometry to the KV cache, the model’s cross-position memory mechanism, and would motivate distributed parameter-decomposition analyses rather than relying on single-head ablation. The current geometry does not establish this mechanism; it points to the experiment.

Continuations: What I Want to De-risk

The reasoning-corpus run is one of several possible continuations, not a foregone conclusion. Before running it, I especially want feedback on the confounds below and on whether the proposed discriminator is the right next instrument.

What the method can see

The timescale estimator is a slow-state detector. It is worth being precise about what that includes and excludes, because it bounds every interpretation above. A high-\(\tau\) direction can be one of several things, and they are not equally interesting.

Held state. The value along the direction sits at a steady level over a passage and varies between passages. This is what C4 surfaced.

Evolving but decodable state. The value changes across tokens, ramping or accumulating, while staying linearly readable from the same direction. This is a variable the model is computing with, not just holding. It is a small extension of the standard linear-representation picture, not a departure from it: the representation is linear at every instant, and only the static reading of that picture, which treats directions as presence detectors, misses it. The timescale estimator cannot reliably distinguish it from held state, because both can produce high autocorrelation.

Transformation off any fixed axis. A quantity being computed may rotate through representation space so that no single direction tracks it. This is outside what a fixed-direction linear method can see: projection onto any one axis decorrelates and \(\tau\) goes small. Catching it would require a time-varying or lifted decoder, such as a latent linear dynamical system or a Koopman-style view in which a rotating quantity becomes a stable mode.

Proposed reasoning-corpus continuation

The estimator is useful but not sufficient for finding interesting maintained state. A high \(\tau\) does not separate a register detector from a live reasoning variable. One next experiment is to run the same pipeline on a corpus that forces maintained abstract computation, such as multi-step proofs, long-horizon code, or controlled reasoning tasks with labeled running variables: a count, a derivation depth, or an accumulating truth value. That run should also add sentence-level shuffling and paraphrase controls to separate maintained state from local lexical clustering more sharply.

The held-versus-evolving discriminator is simple: regress each slow direction’s within-document projection trajectory against the labeled running quantity. If the projection tracks a document-level constant, it is held state; if it tracks the running value as the computation progresses, the slow subspace carries a variable mid-computation. This tests whether the slow subspace is a curiosity about web-document register or a window onto reasoning.

A second, smaller future experiment follows from the geometry: separately measure timescale in the PCA-256 projection of the slow basis and in its orthogonal remainder, to determine whether the temporal state rides on the PCA-captured half or on the roughly half of the basis energy that lies outside PCA-256. That requires projection chunks or a rerun and is out of scope here.

Appendix A: Time-lagged probe estimator

Let \(r_{d,t}^{(\ell)} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{model}}}\) be the residual stream for document \(d\), token \(t\), and hook layer \(\ell\). Train-token centering uses

\[ \mu = \mathbb{E}_{d,t \in \mathrm{train}}[r_{d,t}^{(\ell)}], \qquad \bar r_{d,t} = r_{d,t}^{(\ell)} - \mu. \]

Validation/test tokens are not used to fit PCA directions, time-lagged probes, or centering statistics.

Timescale estimator

For a unit probe \(\hat v_j\),

\[ P_{d,t,j} = \hat v_j^\top r_{d,t}^{(\ell)}. \]

Within-document demeaning:

\[ \tilde P_{d,t,j} = P_{d,t,j} - \frac{1}{T} \sum_{u=0}^{T-1} P_{d,u,j}. \]

Per-document lag-\(k\) autocorrelation:

\[ r_{j,d}(k) = \operatorname{corr} \left( \tilde P_{d,0:T-k,j}, \tilde P_{d,k:T,j} \right). \]

Probe-level curve, with equal document weighting:

\[ R_j(k) = \frac{1}{|\mathcal D_{j,k}|} \sum_{d \in \mathcal D_{j,k}} r_{j,d}(k), \]

where \(\mathcal D_{j,k}\) excludes document/probe/lag entries with near-zero lag-slice variance. Pilot requires \(|\mathcal D_{j,k}| \geq 100\).

I smooth \(R_j(k)\) with a centered moving average of width 5 for \(k \geq 1\), set \(R_j(0)=1\), and clip to \([-1,1]\). The reported timescale is

\[ \tau_j = \min\{k \geq 1 : R_j(k) < 1/e\}. \]

If no crossing occurs by \(K=512\), the probe is marked right-censored. In this pilot, no non-random probes were right-censored.

Time-lagged probe fitting

The time-lagged probes are fit on train documents only. Estimate

\[ \Sigma_0 = \mathbb{E}[\bar r_t \bar r_t^\top], \qquad \Sigma_k = \mathbb{E}[\bar r_t \bar r_{t+k}^\top]. \]

Use the symmetrized multi-lag covariance

\[ \Sigma_{\mathrm{lag}} = \sum_{k \in \{8,16,32,64,128\}} \frac{\Sigma_k+\Sigma_k^\top}{2}. \]

Time-lagged probes are the leading generalized eigenvectors of

\[ \Sigma_{\mathrm{lag}} v = \lambda(\Sigma_0+\epsilon I)v, \]

Conceptually, this maximizes a direction’s multi-lag self-predictiveness, \(\frac{v^\top \Sigma_{\mathrm{lag}} v}{v^\top(\Sigma_0+\epsilon I)v}\): the numerator rewards covariance with future positions, while the denominator prevents the estimator from merely selecting high-variance directions.

with

\[ \epsilon = 10^{-4} \frac{\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_0)}{d_{\mathrm{model}}}. \]

If \(\operatorname{cond}(\Sigma_0+\epsilon I)>10^4\), \(\epsilon\) is doubled until the condition number falls below \(10^4\), up to \(10^{-1}\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_0)/d_{\mathrm{model}}\).

The covariance objective chooses candidate directions; the held-out \(1/e\) autocorrelation crossing defines the reported timescale. All reported \(\tau\) values use this same within-document estimator. Their scale differs because they summarize different probe sets: the headline Q90 of 17 is computed across the original time-lagged probes, while the subspace battery in Appendix E samples rotations within the selected \(Q_{31}\) span and nested prefixes concentrated on its slowest directions.

This objective has standard precedents. It is closely related to slow feature analysis (Wiskott and Sejnowski, 2002) and to time-lagged independent component analysis (Perez-Hernandez et al., 2013), which extracts slow collective coordinates through a lagged-covariance generalized eigenproblem. TICA has a variational interpretation in terms of slow transfer-operator modes, making operator language a useful analogy. I use the narrower claim here: this probe fit surfaces slow linear residual-stream modes; it is not a full Koopman or dynamic-mode-decomposition analysis.

Appendix B: Held-out projection-collapse method

Let \(G_k\) be the top-\(k\) eligible residual probes ranked by held-out within-document timescale after deduplication. I orthonormalize these directions to obtain a slow-direction basis \(Q_k\).

For each residual vector, I remove the component in this basis:

\[ r^{\perp k}_{d,t} = r_{d,t} - Q_kQ_k^\top r_{d,t}. \]

I then recompute held-out probe projections,

\[ P^{\perp k}_{d,t,j} = v_j^\top r^{\perp k}_{d,t}, \]

and re-estimate the same within-document autocorrelation timescale \(\tau_j\) used in the main analysis.

The held-out evaluation probes are fixed before projection-collapse evaluation:

  • \(J_{\mathrm{random}}\): fresh random unit directions, independent of the random probes eligible for \(G_k\);
  • \(J_{\mathrm{lag\text{-}heldout}}\): independently fit time-lagged probes, fit on disjoint train documents from the candidate time-lagged probes and never eligible for \(G_k\).

The candidate time-lagged probes used to construct \(G_k\) are fit on train shard A. The held-out time-lagged evaluation probes are fit on train shard B. Both are evaluated on the same validation/test documents. This makes the test non-circular: the evaluation probes are not used to construct the projected basis, but their before/after timescales are measured on the same held-out documents.

Projection-collapse split details

The corpus contained 5,000 C4 documents of length 1,024 tokens:

Bucket Documents Tokens
Full train split 4,000 4,096,000
Train shard A 3,200 3,276,800
Train shard B 800 819,200
Validation 500 512,000
Test 500 512,000

For the projection-collapse analysis, the train split was further partitioned using a held-out train fraction of 0.2. Candidate time-lagged probes used to construct the projected basis were fit on train shard A. The 128 held-out time-lagged evaluation probes, \(J_{\mathrm{lag\text{-}heldout}}\), were independently fit on train shard B and were never eligible for the projected basis.

Validation and test projection-collapse evaluations used the same fixed probes and bases without refitting. The current Stage 05 summary file contains the final test-only confirmation because the test rerun overwrote the output table, but the pilot run summary records that projection collapse was evaluated on both validation and test.

For each held-out evaluation family \(J_\star\), the collapse score is

\[ C(k) = 1 - \frac{ \operatorname{median}_{j \in J_\star} [ \tau_j(r^{\perp k}) ] }{ \operatorname{median}_{j \in J_\star} [ \tau_j(r) ] }. \]

I compute \(C(k)\) for three matched bases:

  1. the slow-direction basis;
  2. the top residual PCA basis;
  3. a random orthonormal control basis.

The random-control basis tests whether collapse is caused merely by deleting any \(k\) dimensions. The PCA basis tests whether held-out persistence functionally depends on high-variance residual geometry.

The test split reuses the same fixed candidate and held-out probes. No candidate or held-out probes are refit for test confirmation.

Appendix C: Scale calibration for unitless overlap scores

For unitless overlap/excess scores \(S(v)\), I report scale relative to matched random residual directions:

\[ \mu_{\mathrm{rand}} = \operatorname{mean}_{v \in \mathrm{random}} S(v), \qquad \sigma_{\mathrm{rand}} = \operatorname{sd}_{v \in \mathrm{random}} S(v), \]

\[ z_{\mathrm{rand}}(S) = \frac{S_{\mathrm{group}}-\mu_{\mathrm{rand}}}{\sigma_{\mathrm{rand}}}. \]

This is a scale calibration, not a Gaussian significance test.

Appendix D: Semantic readout procedure

The semantic readout is exploratory and is not used in locked quantitative results.

For slow direction \(v_j\), compute held-out validation projections:

\[ P_{d,t,j}=v_j^\top r_{d,t}^{(\ell)}. \]

For the document-coverage comparison, I apply the same procedure to ten sampled vectors from each span family. For each direction, I take the global q95 projection threshold, retain contiguous above-threshold token runs of at least 8 tokens, and collapse them to distinct validation documents. This asks how widely a direction sustains a long high-projection run without letting repeated runs from one page inflate the count. I also report the number of retained runs per covered document.

As a separate window-independence check, I count distinct source documents among the top 12 projection windows. A lower count can be the expected signature of document-state rather than an inflation artifact: a direction that tracks document-level state should produce correlated windows within a page. The distinct-document run count is the safeguard against mistaking one repeated page for a corpus-wide effect.

Finally, I inspect readable windows around upper- and lower-tail token positions of \(P_{d,t,j}\). Labels summarize recurring patterns in those tail examples.

These are qualitative projection-tail labels, not trained classifiers, causal features, or validated semantic variables. For example, “legal/privacy boilerplate” means that many tail examples had that style; it does not mean the direction exclusively encodes that concept.

Appendix E: Slow-subspace battery

This appendix details the diagnostics behind Finding 5. All quantities are evaluated on held-out validation and test documents using the timescale estimator of Appendix A. The battery operates on saved projection artifacts; it performs no additional model forward pass or probe refit beyond the sampling described here.

Random-in-span sampling. Let \(Q_{31}\) be the orthonormalized basis of the 31 directions from Finding 4. To sample a random unit vector inside the span, I draw \(g \sim N(0, I_{31})\), form \(v = Q_{31}\,g\), and normalize to unit ambient norm. This gives a vector in \(\mathbb{R}^{2304}\) lying exactly in \(\mathrm{span}(Q_{31})\), uniform on the unit sphere of that subspace. I sample 512 such directions and compute \(\tau\) for each.

Matched controls. The same procedure is applied to the top-\(k\) PCA subspaces for \(k \in \{31, 128, 256\}\) and to the full ambient space. The PCA-31 control is the critical matched-dimensional comparator: it tests whether selecting the same number of highest-variance residual axes is enough to recover a generically slow subspace. PCA-128 and PCA-256 test whether random rotations become slow merely by drawing them from progressively broader high-variance residual geometry.

Classification rule (pre-registered). The slow subspace is classified “fat” when the median random-in-span \(\tau\) exceeds the ambient-random Q90 null. The result is fat: median random-in-span \(\tau = 24\) (val), \(25\) (test) against an ambient null of \(1\). The stronger “coordinate-free” claim, that essentially every direction in the span is slow, was not certified by this rule, because the locked rule tests only the median. The lower-tail analysis below is the empirical strengthening toward that stronger statement, but it is reported as exploratory rather than as a locked result.

Nested dimension sweep. Random-in-span sampling is repeated restricted to the top-\(k\) directions for \(k \in \{1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 21, 31\}\). Median \(\tau\) declines with \(k\) (\(492\), \(298\), \(201\), \(116\), \(87\), \(30\), \(27.5\), \(25\) on test). The decline is partly mechanical, since a larger span admits progressively less-slow directions. The two non-mechanical observations are that the floor at \(k=31\) (\(\tau=25\)) stays far above the null, and that random mixes of only ranks 17 to 31 retain median \(\tau = 14\) (val \(13\)).

Participation ratios. For the 31 directions I report two ratios using the participation-ratio functional \((\sum_i x_i)^2 / \sum_i x_i^2\). Applied to the covariance eigenvalues of the direction set it gives the geometric participation ratio, \(28.2\), confirming near-orthogonality and close-to-full rank. Applied to the timescale-excess values \(\Delta\tau_i\) it gives the slowness participation ratio, \(4.7\), quantifying that persistence concentrates into roughly five effective dimensions despite the high geometric rank.

Lower-tail analysis. With a 5000-replicate direction bootstrap, the fraction of the 512 random-in-span directions exceeding the ambient Q95 null is \(1.0\), with quantiles q05 = \(10\), q25 = \(18\), q50 = \(25\), q75 = \(40\), q95 = \(82\) (test). Matched controls: random PCA-31 has 50% above the null, PCA-128 has 29%, PCA-256 has 15%. The bootstrap characterizes Monte Carlo uncertainty over the already-sampled directions and does not substitute for a fresh prospective split.

Split stability. The 31 selected directions have median \(\tau\) of \(17\) (train), \(16\) (val), \(17\) (test), with Spearman correlations of \(0.99\) across all split pairs and a train-to-test median shrinkage ratio of \(1.0\). The random-in-span endpoint reproduces across val and test with Spearman \(0.996\) and median absolute \(\tau\) change of 1, with the fraction above the per-split null equal to \(1.0\) on both. This rules out the shrinkage regime in which directions are slow on the fit shard and regress to the null on held-out data.

Disclosure. The test split had been inspected elsewhere in the workflow before the battery rule was locked, so test results are formally exploratory. Validation is the independent held-out reference and tells the same story.
